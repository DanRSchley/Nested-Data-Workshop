A Fully Worked Multilevel Modeling Example

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code

A student brings us a result: transparent fee disclosure appears to increase adoption intent. The p-value is small. The plot looks clean. But before we believe the estimate, we audit the data. We find seven threats: repeated observations, sampled stimuli, treatment-risk imbalance, nonlinear product valuation and fatigue, differential missingness, post-treatment exclusion risk, and treatment-effect heterogeneity.

The rest of this chapter diagnoses where each threat comes from, which adjustment reduces it, and what remains when we are done.

The key framing: Statistical fixes minimize threats — they do not erase them. The goal is not to find an analysis that says everything was fine. It is to document what each adjustment addressed, what it could not address, and what the final claim depends on.

You do not need to read every paragraph to follow the logic. Each step answers one diagnostic question.

1. The Research Question and Estimand

Does transparent fee disclosure increase willingness to adopt a financial product, and does that effect generalize across participants, product offers, and research sites?

The target estimand is the average total effect of assigned fee transparency on willingness to adopt, averaged over the population of participants, product offers, and contexts similar to those sampled. This is an intention-to-treat estimand: the effect of the display assignment, not the effect of comprehension or perceived clarity. Comprehension and perceived transparency are potential mechanisms and must be excluded from the main model.

2. Design Audit Before Modeling

What is one row? One participant’s rating of one product Trial-level outcome; multiple rows per participant

Is assignment perfectly randomized? No — correlated with risk, trial order, site 2 Covariate adjustment needed

3. Simulate the Latent DGP

The true data-generating process is known. The realized data will not look like it because: (1) the fee-transparency display was more commonly served to high-risk products in later trials — a real implementation pattern; (2) trial-order fatigue is real; (3) the observation process introduces non-random missingness and post-treatment attention failures.

Show DGP parameters 
dgp <- list(
  intercept           = 50,
  effect_transparency = 4,      # +4 at average literacy — the target
  effect_risk         = -2.0,   # linear risk penalty
  effect_risk_high    = -2.5,   # threshold penalty for risk > 0.75
  effect_ev_linear    = 2.2,    # EV (standardised)
  effect_ev_quad      = -1.0,   # diminishing returns
  effect_literacy     = 2,
  interaction_tx_lit  = -1.5,   # transparency helps low-literacy more
  effect_fatigue      = -0.18,  # per trial
  effect_fatigue_late = -1.20,  # extra penalty for trials 10–12
  effect_device       = -1.5,   # mobile: lower ratings
  sd_participant      = 8,
  sd_stimulus         = 5,
  sd_site             = 4,
  sd_slope_part       = 2,
  sd_residual         = 7
)
Show simulation code (participants, stimuli, trials) 
n_participants  <- 240
n_stimuli       <- 36
n_sites         <- 4
trials_per_part <- 12

participants <- tibble(
  participant_id     = 1:n_participants,
  site_id            = rep(1:n_sites, each = n_participants / n_sites),
  financial_literacy = rnorm(n_participants, 0, 1),
  u0_participant     = rnorm(n_participants, 0, dgp$sd_participant),
  u1_participant     = rnorm(n_participants, 0, dgp$sd_slope_part)
)

site_effects <- tibble(
  site_id = 1:n_sites,
  u0_site = c(3, -2, 1, -2)
)

stimuli <- tibble(
  stimulus_id    = 1:n_stimuli,
  risk_level     = rnorm(n_stimuli, 0, 1),
  expected_value = rnorm(n_stimuli, 50, 15),
  u0_stimulus    = rnorm(n_stimuli, 0, dgp$sd_stimulus)
) |>
  mutate(
    expected_value_z = (expected_value - 50) / 15,
    risk_high        = as.integer(risk_level > 0.75)
  )

trials <- participants |>
  select(participant_id, site_id, financial_literacy, u0_participant, u1_participant) |>
  cross_join(tibble(draw = 1:trials_per_part)) |>
  group_by(participant_id) |>
  mutate(
    stimulus_id   = sample(1:n_stimuli, trials_per_part, replace = FALSE),
    trial_order   = 1:trials_per_part
  ) |>
  ungroup() |>
  select(-draw) |>
  left_join(stimuli, by = "stimulus_id") |>
  left_join(site_effects, by = "site_id") |>
  mutate(
    device_mobile = rbinom(n(), 1, plogis(-0.3 + 0.6 * as.integer(site_id %in% c(2, 3)))),

    # IMPERFECT ASSIGNMENT: transparent display more common for high-risk products,
    # later trial positions, and at site 2 — a realistic platform rollout pattern
    trial_order_z = (trial_order - 6.5) / 3.452,
    p_tx          = plogis(0.70 * risk_level + 0.40 * trial_order_z +
                             0.50 * as.integer(site_id == 2)),
    transparency  = rbinom(n(), 1, p_tx),

    mu = dgp$intercept +
         dgp$effect_transparency  * transparency +
         dgp$effect_risk          * risk_level +
         dgp$effect_risk_high     * risk_high +
         dgp$effect_ev_linear     * expected_value_z +
         dgp$effect_ev_quad       * expected_value_z^2 +
         dgp$effect_literacy      * financial_literacy +
         dgp$interaction_tx_lit   * transparency * financial_literacy +
         dgp$effect_fatigue       * trial_order +
         dgp$effect_fatigue_late  * as.integer(trial_order > 9) +
         dgp$effect_device        * device_mobile +
         u0_participant + u1_participant * transparency +
         u0_stimulus + u0_site,

    resid_sd = dgp$sd_residual * exp(
      0.25 * abs(risk_level) + 0.20 * as.integer(financial_literacy < -1)
    ),
    adoption_intent = pmin(100, pmax(0, rnorm(n(), mu, resid_sd)))
  ) |>
  select(participant_id, site_id, stimulus_id, trial_order,
         transparency, risk_level, risk_high, expected_value, expected_value_z,
         financial_literacy, device_mobile, adoption_intent)

Result: The naive estimate is 1.83 — 2.17 points below the true effect of 4. It is significant, which is exactly what makes it dangerous.

Why is the estimate too low?

  • Treatment–risk imbalance: transparent trials go to high-risk products; high risk reduces adoption → transparency is blamed for the risk penalty.
  • Trial-order confound: transparent trials occur later on average; late trials have lower ratings due to fatigue → transparency is also blamed for fatigue.
  • Clustering ignored: the same participants and products appear repeatedly, inflating apparent precision.
  • Missingness ignored: 468 rows were dropped without comment; these are not a random subsample.

All threats are active. The estimate is far from the true effect because structural, confounding, and missingness problems are all compounded.

Open: participant dependence, stimulus heterogeneity, treatment–risk imbalance, nonlinear valuation/fatigue, differential missingness, post-treatment exclusions, effect heterogeneity.

8. Participant Random Intercepts: Fix Repeated-Person Dependence

NoteAt a Glance
  • Open threat: Participant dependence — same person appears 12 times.
  • Evidence: Each participant has a stable adoption tendency. Treating rows as independent ignores this.
  • Model change: Add (1 | participant_id).
  • What to watch: The participant ICC and how the SE changes. The estimate should not move much — participant RI addresses dependence, not the stimulus-level risk confounding.
Code 
m1 <- lmer(adoption_intent_obs ~ transparency + (1 | participant_id), data = trials)
Code

  • What this fixed: person-to-person baseline differences are no longer in the residual.
  • Why the estimate did not move: the main bias is stimulus-level (transparent trials go to risky products). Participant RI does not adjust for that.
  • What is still open: product-level confounding is the main problem — that comes next.

9. Stimulus Random Intercepts: Generalize and Absorb Product Confounding

NoteAt a Glance
  • Open threat: Stimulus heterogeneity and (partly) treatment–risk imbalance.
  • Evidence: Product-level variation is large; stimulus SD is expected to exceed the transparency effect.
  • Model change: Add (1 | stimulus_id).
  • What to watch: Stimulus SD relative to the transparency effect. The estimate is expected to jump because risk is a stimulus-level variable, and the stimulus RI absorbs product-level attractiveness differences — including the risk-related part.

Why the estimate jumped — two reasons:

  1. Generalization. Stimuli are now treated as a sample, not as fixed objects. The claim is about the population of products, not just these 36.

  2. Product-level confounding absorbed. This is the more important reason for the estimate jump. Risk level is a stimulus-level variable: each product has one risk value, and all participants who see that product see the same risk level. Because transparent trials disproportionately involved high-risk products, the stimulus random intercept — which gives each product its own baseline — automatically absorbs the product attractiveness differences that were confounding the transparency estimate. The estimate jumped not because we added risk as an explicit covariate, but because we gave every product its own intercept.

Why covariates are still needed: The stimulus random intercept makes a flexible, non-parametric adjustment. Adding explicit risk covariates does three things the random intercept cannot: (1) makes the adjustment interpretable — “riskier products had lower adoption”; (2) extrapolates to products not in this sample using the estimated risk slope; (3) captures within-product nonlinear effects (a quadratic EV effect cannot be absorbed by a product-level intercept shift). These come in m4 and m4b.

NoteLesson: Random Intercepts and Covariates Are Not Alternatives

  • Without the covariate, the RI cannot tell us why products differ in their baselines or how to generalize.

The small estimate movement at m4 is not a failure. It is the expected pattern when a stimulus RI correctly absorbed the cluster-level confounding at m2.

20. What We Fixed vs What We Did Not Fix

What the analysis substantially reduced

  • Row dependence — participant and stimulus random intercepts correctly handle clustering.
  • Product-level assignment imbalance — stimulus random intercepts absorbed the largest part of the product-risk confounding at m2; explicit covariates made it interpretable at m4.
  • Functional-form misspecification — nonlinear risk and EV terms corrected the remaining bias from the risk threshold and diminishing returns.
  • Trial-order fatigue — adding trial order removed confounding from transparent trials occurring later.
  • Observed differential missingness — the device covariate adjusted for an additional missingness predictor.

What the analysis did not fully solve

  • MNAR — if latent adoption intent predicts dropout beyond all observed covariates, no adjustment in this chapter addresses it. The sensitivity was reassuring; the assumption is still an assumption.
  • Post-treatment selection — the attention-exclusion sensitivity showed that excluding failures narrows the estimand. The primary model does not exclude; the sensitivity model does. Neither is definitively correct.
  • Limited site generalization — with four sites, the site random intercept is estimated with minimal precision. Treatment effect heterogeneity across sites cannot be reliably detected.
  • Heteroskedasticity — residuals fan with risk level, violating the LMM assumption of constant variance. For high-risk stimuli, confidence intervals may be too narrow.

The honest final claim

After diagnosing and removing the major observed threats — product-level assignment imbalance, nonlinear covariate effects, and trial-order fatigue — fee transparency appears to increase adoption intent by approximately 3.8 points at average financial literacy (true DGP: 4), with larger benefits for lower-literacy participants. This estimate is meaningfully more defensible than the naive result of 1.8, but it still depends on assumptions about missingness, post-treatment exclusions, and the representativeness of stimuli and sites.

21. What a Reviewer Would Ask

1. “The transparency coefficient predicting assignment is 0.74 in your audit. Was this truly randomized?”

No — not perfectly. The transparency display was served more often to high-risk products and later trial positions. We documented this in the assignment audit and removed it by (a) stimulus random intercepts, which absorbed the product-level confounding, and (b) nonlinear risk and trial-order covariates, which captured the within-stimulus residual. The estimate moved substantially as a result.

2. “You say stimulus random intercepts absorbed the product-level confounding. Isn’t that circularity?”

No. Risk level predicts both transparency assignment and adoption intent. The stimulus random intercept gives each product a flexible baseline — whatever predicts the product-level attractiveness, including risk, is absorbed. We then added explicit risk covariates to make that adjustment interpretable and to handle within-product nonlinear effects. The combination is more informative than either alone: the RI handles flexible product baselines; the covariates name the mechanism.

3. “Your m4 estimate barely moved from m3. Why call it a useful step?”

Because it serves a different purpose. The product-level adjustment was already handled by the stimulus RI at m2. m4’s contribution is interpretability — we now know that riskier products drove most of the product-level attractiveness differences, and we have an estimate of the linear risk slope. That matters for generalizing beyond this sample of 36 products. A model with only a stimulus RI cannot extrapolate; a model with explicit risk covariates can.

4. “16% of outcomes are missing. Your sensitivity analysis moved the estimate by only 0.02. Did missingness really not matter?”

Missingness looked alarming in the audit. The diagnostic sensitivity (adding device_mobile) was small, suggesting that the missingness predictors already in the model (risk, trial order, literacy) accounted for most of the differential dropout. The remaining concern is MNAR: if latent adoption intent predicts dropout above and beyond all observed predictors, no covariate adjustment reaches it. We flag this as a caveat and note that the small observed-sensitivity shift is reassuring but not conclusive.

5. “Why not exclude attention failures? They indicate low data quality.”

Attention failures are 2.5× more common in the transparent condition (12.1% vs 5.4%). If we exclude them, we are preferentially removing trials where the transparent display was most cognitively demanding — which may be the exact trials where the treatment effect is most muted. Exclusion conditions on a post-treatment variable and produces a different estimand: “effect for trials participants processed without difficulty” rather than “effect of being assigned a transparent display.” Both estimands are defensible, but they should not be conflated.

6. “The stimulus SD is about 6.7 — larger than the transparency effect. Does your generalization claim hold?”

Yes, and this is exactly why the stimulus random intercept is essential. If we had treated stimuli as fixed, the SE on the transparency effect would be too small (product-level variance absorbed into the residual inflates false precision). The random intercept correctly partitions product variance from the within-product treatment effect. The large product SD does mean that the specific stimulus sample matters — a very different set of 36 products could give a different average effect. That is an honest limitation of any single-study result.

7. “What is your estimand?”

The average total effect of assigned fee transparency on willingness to adopt, averaged over the population of participants, product offers, and contexts similar to those sampled, at average financial literacy. At other literacy levels, the simple-slope table gives the conditional estimate. This is an ITT estimand — comprehension and perceived transparency are mechanisms, not adjustments.

8. “For whom does the effect hold?”

The literacy interaction (m5) shows that transparency benefits lower-literacy participants more. The random slope (m6b) shows that there is also heterogeneity across participants beyond what literacy captures. With four sites and 36 stimuli, the boundaries of generalization are modest: similar populations, similar financial product types, similar deployment contexts.
