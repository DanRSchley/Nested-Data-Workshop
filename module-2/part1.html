Centering as Decomposition

NoteWhat Does Centering Reveal?
  • Question: A single predictor in nested data carries two different relationships simultaneously — one operating within units (how does savings change when this household engages more with disclosure this month?) and one operating between units (do households that engage more overall save more?). Which one does your model estimate?
  • What goes wrong: An uncentered predictor in a random-effects model blends both mechanisms into a single coefficient. If the within-unit and between-unit effects differ in size or sign, this blended estimate is wrong for both questions — and useless for both policy and theory.
  • The fix: Cluster-mean centering (CMC) separates the predictor into a within-unit deviation (from the household’s own average) and a between-unit mean. The model then estimates both effects simultaneously, allowing you to determine whether the same mechanism operates at both levels.

One Variable, Two Mechanisms

When you measure “hours of sleep” across multiple days from multiple people, that variable carries two completely different stories at the same time:

  1. The within-person story: On days when this person sleeps more than they usually do, how does their mood change?
  2. The between-person story: Do people who sleep more overall have higher mood than people who sleep less?

These are different empirical questions. They may have different answers. They probably operate through different mechanisms — the within-person effect may reflect acute physiological recovery, while the between-person effect may reflect stable personality traits (conscientious people both sleep more and have better mood, not because sleep causes mood but because conscientiousness causes both).

Centering is not a cosmetic choice about where the intercept falls. It is a decision about which mechanism you are modeling — and whether you want to see both.

The Equations: From Conflation to Decomposition

The Conflated Model

When you include an uncentered (or grand-mean-centered) predictor in a random-effects model, a single coefficient \(\beta_1\) blends two mechanisms:

\[ \underbrace{Y_{ij}}_{\text{outcome}} = \underbrace{\gamma_{00}}_{\substack{\text{grand} \\ \text{mean}}} + \underbrace{\beta_1}_{\substack{\text{blended} \\ \text{slope}}} \underbrace{X_{ij}}_{\substack{\text{predictor}}} + \underbrace{u_{0j}}_{\substack{\text{person} \\ \text{RE}}} + \underbrace{\varepsilon_{ij}}_{\substack{\text{residual}}} \]

\(\beta_1\) absorbs variation from two sources simultaneously. Its value depends on which source dominates and how the two correlate.

The Decomposition

CMC separates the predictor into its two components before entering the model:

\[ \underbrace{X_{ij}}_{\substack{\text{observed} \\ \text{predictor}}} = \underbrace{(X_{ij} - \bar{X}_{j})}_{\substack{\text{within-unit} \\ \text{deviation}}} + \underbrace{\bar{X}_{j}}_{\substack{\text{unit mean} \\ \text{(between)}}} \]

Adding both as separate predictors gives the decomposed model. Notice how the single \(\beta_1\) from before splits into two:

\[ \underbrace{Y_{ij}}_{\text{outcome}} = \underbrace{\gamma_{00}}_{\substack{\text{grand} \\ \text{mean}}} + \underbrace{\beta_W}_{\substack{\text{within-unit} \\ \text{slope}}} \underbrace{(X_{ij} - \bar{X}_j)}_{\substack{\text{deviation from} \\ \text{own mean}}} + \underbrace{\beta_B}_{\substack{\text{between-unit} \\ \text{slope}}} \underbrace{\bar{X}_j}_{\substack{\text{unit's} \\ \text{average level}}} + \underbrace{u_{0j}}_{\substack{\text{person} \\ \text{RE}}} + \underbrace{\varepsilon_{ij}}_{\substack{\text{residual}}} \]

Now \(\beta_W\) answers the within-person question and \(\beta_B\) answers the between-person question. They are allowed to be completely different numbers with different interpretations.

NoteThe DGP Interpretation

The decomposed model is a hypothesis about the DGP: it asserts that two distinct mechanisms are at work, one operating at the within-unit level and one at the between-unit level. If \(\beta_W \neq \beta_B\), the DGP truly has two mechanisms. If \(\beta_W \approx \beta_B\), a single mechanism may span both levels — but you only know this after estimating the decomposed model and testing whether they differ.

Why Not Just Use Pooled Regression?

ImportantThe Case Against Pooled OLS (and Uncentered Random Effects)

A natural question: what is wrong with running lm(savings ~ disclosure, data = df) or lmer(savings ~ disclosure + (1 | household), data = df) without centering? Both produce a single coefficient. Both are easy to fit. Neither requires any centering decisions.

The answer: both conflate two mechanisms that may differ in sign and magnitude, making the coefficient uninterpretable for any specific question.

Model What the coefficient estimates When it goes wrong
Pooled OLS Blend of within- and between-person effects When between-person sorting confounds the within-person relationship (almost always in panel data)
Uncentered random-effects Same blended coefficient When the RE assumption (random effects ⊥ predictor) fails — which it usually does when the predictor varies between persons
CMC decomposition \(\beta_W\) (within) and \(\beta_B\) (between) separately It doesn’t “go wrong” — it reveals both effects and makes the question explicit

The key question to ask before fitting any model with panel data: Does the predictor vary both within and between units? If yes — almost always the case for continuous predictors like disclosure engagement, app usage, or spending — pooled OLS and uncentered RE give you a blended estimate that is wrong for both the causal within-unit question and the descriptive between-unit question.

Centering is not a technicality. It is the only way to know which question your model is answering.

Three Archetypal Patterns

The most important practical question is: how different are \(\beta_W\) and \(\beta_B\)?

Pattern 1: Convergent Effects — Budgeting App Engagement and Savings Rate

Both mechanisms point in the same direction with similar magnitude. On weeks when a customer opens the budgeting app more than their own typical frequency (within-person), they save more that week — the reflection and goal-salience triggered by app engagement directly improves saving behavior. And across customers, those who engage with the app more on average (between-person) also save more overall — they are both more financially motivated and get more cumulative benefit from the tool. True: \(\beta_W \approx \beta_B \approx 1.5\).

Code 
n_ppl <- 60; n_weeks <- 10

person_app_avg  <- rnorm(n_ppl, 5, 1.5)    # typical weekly app opens
person_sav_base <- 20 + 2 * person_app_avg + rnorm(n_ppl, 0, 4)  # motivated users save more

df_app <- expand_grid(person = 1:n_ppl, week = 1:n_weeks) |>
  mutate(
    app_opens = pmax(0, person_app_avg[person] + rnorm(n(), 0, 2)),
    savings   = person_sav_base[person] +
                1.5 * (app_opens - person_app_avg[person]) +
                rnorm(n(), 0, 4),
    person    = factor(person)
  ) |>
  group_by(person) |>
  mutate(app_mean = mean(app_opens), app_cmc = app_opens - app_mean) |>
  ungroup()

m_app <- lmer(savings ~ app_cmc + app_mean + (1 | person), data = df_app)
broom.mixed::tidy(m_app, effects = "fixed") |> filter(term != "(Intercept)") |>
  mutate(mechanism = c("Within-person (app_cmc)", "Between-person (app_mean)")) |>
  select(mechanism, estimate, std.error, p.value) |>
  knitr::kable(digits = 3,
    caption = "Pattern 1: within ≈ between ≈ 1.5. One coherent mechanism at two levels — pooled OLS approximates well here, but you only know that after checking.")
Pattern 1: within ≈ between ≈ 1.5. One coherent mechanism at two levels — pooled OLS approximates well here, but you only know that after checking.
mechanism estimate std.error p.value
Within-person (app_cmc) 1.477 0.099 0
Between-person (app_mean) 1.745 0.336 0

Here, the pooled model gives a reasonable approximation — but you only know that after running the decomposition and finding convergence. The convergence itself is substantively informative: the app appears to operate through the same mechanism at both timescales (goal activation and financial awareness).

Pattern 2: Divergent Effects — Weekly Transactions and Monthly Savings Rate

A common misconception in financial data: customers who transact more frequently appear to save more (between-customer relationship). But within a customer, weeks when they make more transactions than usual correspond to lower savings that month — more spending activity drains current savings. The between-person relationship is positive because financially engaged customers are both more active AND more organized about saving. The within-person relationship is negative because extra transactions this week represent extra spending. True: \(\beta_W \approx -0.8\) (within: more transactions → less saved), \(\beta_B \approx +2.0\) (between: active customers save more).

Code 
n_ppl2 <- 80; n_months <- 8

person_tx_avg   <- rnorm(n_ppl2, 20, 6)              # typical monthly transactions
person_sav_base <- 15 + 2.0 * person_tx_avg + rnorm(n_ppl2, 0, 5)  # engaged customers save more

df_tx <- expand_grid(person = 1:n_ppl2, month = 1:n_months) |>
  mutate(
    transactions = pmax(2, person_tx_avg[person] + rnorm(n(), 0, 5)),
    savings_rate = person_sav_base[person] +
                   (-0.8) * (transactions - person_tx_avg[person]) +   # more spending → less saving
                   rnorm(n(), 0, 4),
    person       = factor(person)
  ) |>
  group_by(person) |>
  mutate(tx_mean = mean(transactions), tx_cmc = transactions - tx_mean) |>
  ungroup()

m_tx     <- lmer(savings_rate ~ tx_cmc + tx_mean + (1 | person), data = df_tx)
m_tx_pool <- lmer(savings_rate ~ transactions + (1 | person), data = df_tx)

bind_rows(
  broom.mixed::tidy(m_tx_pool, effects = "fixed") |>
    filter(term == "transactions") |> mutate(model = "Pooled (blended)"),
  broom.mixed::tidy(m_tx, effects = "fixed") |>
    filter(term == "tx_cmc") |> mutate(model = "Within-person"),
  broom.mixed::tidy(m_tx, effects = "fixed") |>
    filter(term == "tx_mean") |> mutate(model = "Between-person")
) |>
  select(model, estimate, std.error, p.value) |>
  knitr::kable(digits = 3,
    caption = "Pattern 2: pooled ≈ +0.4 (near zero or positive); within ≈ −0.8 (spending crowds out saving); between ≈ +2.0 (engaged customers save more). Pooled is misleading for both questions.")
Pattern 2: pooled ≈ +0.4 (near zero or positive); within ≈ −0.8 (spending crowds out saving); between ≈ +2.0 (engaged customers save more). Pooled is misleading for both questions.
model estimate std.error p.value
Pooled (blended) -0.726 0.032 0
Within-person -0.755 0.033 0
Between-person 1.885 0.108 0

The pooled model gives a near-zero or weakly positive estimate — a blend of the negative within-customer spending-crowding effect and the positive between-customer engagement effect. A product team concluding “more transactions → more savings” (because the pooled slope is positive) would be wrong: within customers, more spending activity in a given month reduces that month’s savings rate.

Pattern 3: Sign Reversal — Price Level and Purchase Rate Across Market Segments

Within a market segment, promotional price reductions (prices below the segment’s typical level) increase purchase rates — basic economics. But across segments, premium segments charge higher prices AND have lower purchase rates — because premium products are niche, not because high prices cause purchases. A pooled analysis finds that higher prices predict lower purchase rates. This is true between segments but wrong as a within-segment statement: within every segment, the price-demand relationship is negative (lower relative price → more purchases). True: \(\beta_W \approx -0.4\) (demand elasticity), \(\beta_B \approx -0.6\) (between-segment selection).

Wait — to get a sign reversal we need the between-segment effect to run opposite to within-segment. Here is the JDM version: Discount depth and category conversion rates — high-discount product categories (commodity goods: toilet paper, cleaning supplies) have both higher average discount depth and lower conversion rates (many competing substitutes). Low-discount categories (specialty foods, premium cosmetics) have lower discount depth and higher conversion rates (loyal buyer base). Pooled: deeper average discounts → lower conversion. Within category: deeper-than-average discounts → higher conversion. Sign reversal.

True: \(\beta_W \approx +0.3\) (within-category: promotion → conversion up), \(\beta_B \approx -0.5\) (between-category: commodity categories have high discounts but low loyalty).

Code 
n_brands     <- 200
n_categories <- 5

# Commodity categories: high discount depth, low conversion (many substitutes)
# Premium categories: low discount depth, high conversion (loyal customers)
cat_discount_avg  <- c(22, 18, 14, 10, 6)    # avg discount depth (%) — high → low commodity→premium
cat_loyalty_base  <- c(18, 25, 35, 45, 52)   # baseline conversion rate (%)

df_discount <- tibble(
  brand    = 1:n_brands,
  category = rep(1:n_categories, each = n_brands / n_categories)
) |>
  mutate(
    discount   = cat_discount_avg[category] + rnorm(n_brands, 0, 4),
    conversion = cat_loyalty_base[category] +
                 0.3 * (discount - cat_discount_avg[category]) +   # within-cat: promotions help
                 rnorm(n_brands, 0, 5),
    category   = factor(category,
                        labels = c("Commodity", "Staple", "Mixed", "Specialty", "Premium"))
  ) |>
  group_by(category) |>
  mutate(disc_mean = mean(discount), disc_cmc = discount - disc_mean) |>
  ungroup()

m_disc_decomp <- lmer(conversion ~ disc_cmc + disc_mean + (1 | category), data = df_discount)
m_disc_pooled <- lm(conversion ~ discount, data = df_discount)

bind_rows(
  broom::tidy(m_disc_pooled) |>
    filter(term == "discount") |> mutate(model = "Pooled OLS"),
  broom.mixed::tidy(m_disc_decomp, effects = "fixed") |>
    filter(term == "disc_cmc") |> mutate(model = "Within-category"),
  broom.mixed::tidy(m_disc_decomp, effects = "fixed") |>
    filter(term == "disc_mean") |> mutate(model = "Between-category")
) |>
  select(model, estimate, std.error, p.value) |>
  knitr::kable(digits = 3,
    caption = "Pattern 3: pooled is negative; within is positive; between is strongly negative. Sign reversal!")
Pattern 3: pooled is negative; within is positive; between is strongly negative. Sign reversal!

The pooled model says promotions hurt conversion — which is wrong. The decomposition reveals the truth: within every category, discounts increase purchase rates; but commodity categories (with high average discounts) also have low inherent loyalty. Ignoring the nesting produces a Simpson’s Paradox (see Part 3).

The Disclosure–Savings Example: Applying Decomposition

50 households complete a monthly financial diary for 7 months. Each month, we record how many pages of the financial disclosure document they reviewed and their savings rate. Two questions are entangled:

  1. Within-household: On months when a household reads more than their own usual amount, do they save more?
  2. Between-household: Do households that engage more with disclosure overall have higher savings?

The between-household relationship is confounded by financial literacy: literate households both engage more with disclosure AND save more regardless of engagement. The within-household relationship is the one that matters for the causal question about disclosure.

Code 
n_hh   <- 50; n_months <- 7

hh_disc_avg    <- rnorm(n_hh, 6, 2)                   # household average disclosure
hh_savings_base <- 20 + 2 * hh_disc_avg + rnorm(n_hh, 0, 5)  # literacy confound

df_center <- expand_grid(household = 1:n_hh, month = 1:n_months) |>
  mutate(
    disc_raw = hh_disc_avg[household] + rnorm(n(), 0, 1.5),
    savings  = hh_savings_base[household] +
               1.5 * (disc_raw - hh_disc_avg[household]) + rnorm(n(), 0, 4),
    household = factor(household)
  ) |>
  group_by(household) |>
  mutate(disc_mean = mean(disc_raw), disc_cmc = disc_raw - disc_mean) |>
  ungroup() |>
  mutate(disc_gmc = disc_raw - mean(disc_raw))

m_uncentered <- lmer(savings ~ disc_raw  + (1 | household), data = df_center)
m_gmc        <- lmer(savings ~ disc_gmc  + (1 | household), data = df_center)
m_cmc        <- lmer(savings ~ disc_cmc + disc_mean + (1 | household), data = df_center)

bind_rows(
  broom.mixed::tidy(m_uncentered, effects = "fixed") |>
    filter(term == "disc_raw") |>
    mutate(model = "Uncentered", mechanism = "Blended"),
  broom.mixed::tidy(m_gmc, effects = "fixed") |>
    filter(term == "disc_gmc") |>
    mutate(model = "GMC", mechanism = "Blended"),
  broom.mixed::tidy(m_cmc, effects = "fixed") |>
    filter(term == "disc_cmc") |>
    mutate(model = "CMC", mechanism = "Within-household"),
  broom.mixed::tidy(m_cmc, effects = "fixed") |>
    filter(term == "disc_mean") |>
    mutate(model = "CMC", mechanism = "Between-household")
) |>
  select(model, mechanism, estimate, std.error, p.value) |>
  knitr::kable(digits = 3,
    caption = "Disclosure → savings: blended ≈ 3.5; within ≈ 1.5; between ≈ 4.0. Blended estimate is wrong for both questions.")
Disclosure → savings: blended ≈ 3.5; within ≈ 1.5; between ≈ 4.0. Blended estimate is wrong for both questions.
model mechanism estimate std.error p.value
Uncentered Blended 1.666 0.160 0
GMC Blended 1.666 0.160 0
CMC Within-household 1.608 0.175 0
CMC Between-household 1.975 0.403 0

The uncentered and GMC models give ~3.5 — overstating the within-household effect by 2× because financial literacy inflates the between-household correlation. CMC reveals: the within-household causal effect (1.5 points per additional page) is meaningfully smaller than the between-household correlation (4.0 points) driven by literacy. A policy that relies on the blended estimate will project benefits of disclosure programmes that are unrealistically large.

Decision Guide

TipWhich Centering to Use
Goal Centering What you get
Interpret the intercept at a meaningful value GMC Intercept = E[Y] when X = grand mean
Estimate the within-unit effect CMC \(\hat\beta_W\) — deviation from own mean
Separate within and between mechanisms CMC + unit mean \(\hat\beta_W\) and \(\hat\beta_B\)
Test if within = between CMC + unit mean Wald test on equality
Single-level data (no nesting) Either No within/between distinction

Default recommendation for nested data: Always use CMC with the unit mean included. Run it, check whether \(\hat\beta_W \approx \hat\beta_B\), and only then decide whether the pooled model is defensible.

WarningAssumption: No Conflation of Within and Between Effects

Including a predictor without CMC in a random-effects model implicitly assumes \(\beta_W = \beta_B\). When this is wrong — as in Patterns 2 and 3 above — both estimates are biased.

How to test: Fit the CMC model. Compare \(\hat\beta_W\) and \(\hat\beta_B\). A Wald test (see Part 2) tests the null \(H_0: \beta_W = \beta_B\).

The stakes: In the worst case (Pattern 3), conflation completely reverses the direction of the effect.

TipReviewer Challenge: Three Questions About Your Centering Choice

Challenge 1 — “Which question does your coefficient actually answer?” If you report a single coefficient for disclosure engagement without centering, a reviewer will ask: “Is this the within-household effect (what happens when a household reads more than usual) or the between-household effect (whether households with generally higher engagement save more)? Because those are different numbers with different policy implications.” Run the CMC decomposition and report both — or justify why the blended estimate is the correct quantity of interest.

Challenge 2 — “Why are the within- and between-household estimates different?” If \(\hat\beta_W \neq \hat\beta_B\), reviewers will ask you to explain the mechanism. For the disclosure study: the between-household effect is inflated by financial literacy — literate households engage more AND save more regardless. The within-household effect is the pure causal question. Have a substantive explanation for the discrepancy, not just a statistical note that “random effects may be correlated with the predictor.”

Challenge 3 — “Does the sign reversal survive other robustness checks?” For the discount/conversion sign reversal example: a reviewer will ask whether the paradox holds when you control for other category-level characteristics (product shelf life, brand loyalty, advertising spend). If the within-category effect becomes near-zero once you add category-level controls, the pattern may reflect omitted variable bias rather than a genuine sign reversal. Run the model with additional controls at both levels.
