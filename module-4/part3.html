Growth Curve Models

NoteThe Growth Curve Question

What is the scientific question? Growth curve models are for questions about trajectories — how fast something changes, whether change rates differ across people, and whether a predictor shifts the rate of change. If your question is only about mean levels at specific waves, a simpler repeated-measures model suffices.

What does this model assume about time? That each person has their own trajectory described by an intercept (their level at time = 0) and a slope (their rate of change). The model estimates the distribution of those intercepts and slopes across people.

What goes wrong if you ignore individual trajectory variation? A random-intercept-only model forces everyone to change at the same rate. If people genuinely differ in how fast they change — some improving rapidly, others plateauing — the model misspecifies the DGP, produces poor predictions for individuals, and may bias the average slope estimate.

Before Fitting: Three Decisions to Make First

Decision 1 — Is a growth curve model the right tool?

Growth curve models answer: “How does the outcome trajectory unfold, and what explains variation in trajectories?” They are not the right tool for every longitudinal question.

Your question Right tool
Is the group mean higher at wave 3 than wave 1? RM-ANOVA or mixed model with time as a factor
Does a treatment shift the level at one follow-up wave? Mixed model with treatment × wave interaction
How fast does the outcome change, and do people differ in their rate? Growth curve model
Does a treatment affect the rate of change, not just the level? Growth curve model with treatment × slope

Decision 2 — What does time = 0 mean?

The intercept in a growth model is the predicted outcome when time = 0. Time coding determines what the intercept represents — choose it for interpretability.

Time coding Intercept means Best for
0 = first wave Baseline level Intervention studies where pre-treatment level matters
0 = midpoint Mid-study level Reduces intercept-slope correlation; improves estimation
0 = last wave Final-wave level When the endpoint is the primary reference
0 = age (individually varying) Level at a specific age Developmental and aging studies

Decision 3 — How many waves, and are they equally spaced?

Waves What you can reliably estimate
2 Intercept + one slope. Growth curve = change score. No information about curvature.
3 Linear growth. Minimal test for non-linearity.
4+ Quadratic growth, piecewise change, or splines. Recommended for detecting trajectory shape.
Unequal spacing Use exact elapsed time as the time variable — not wave number.

With only 2 waves, a growth curve model adds nothing beyond a difference score. Fit a simpler model.

Trajectories as the Target

Standard repeated measures models ask: “Is the mean outcome different across time points?” Growth curve models ask something richer: “How does each unit’s trajectory unfold over time, and what explains variation in those trajectories?”

The shift in framing is fundamental. We are not trying to estimate one average change — we are trying to recover the distribution of individual change processes that generated the data. Each person has their own DGP; growth curve models estimate the parameters of that distribution.

The Example Dataset

To demonstrate these concepts, we simulate 60 participants each assessed at 6 weekly waves. Each person has their own true baseline stress level and their own true rate of weekly change — the DGP we want the growth model to recover.

Code 
n_people <- 60
n_times  <- 6  # weeks

# Each person has a random baseline and random rate of change
person_intercepts <- rnorm(n_people, 50, 8)
person_slopes     <- rnorm(n_people, -2, 1.5)  # average decline of 2/week

df_growth <- expand_grid(person = 1:n_people, week = 0:(n_times - 1)) |>
  mutate(
    stress = person_intercepts[person] +
             person_slopes[person] * week +
             rnorm(n(), 0, 4),
    person = factor(person)
  )

p_spaghetti | p_means | p_change

What to look for:

  • Spaghetti plot: Do trajectories fan out over time? Wide fan-out signals that individuals differ in their rate of change — a random slope is needed.
  • Mean-by-wave: Does the average look linear? If the means accelerate, decelerate, or level off, a linear model will be misspecified.
  • Individual change plot: Is the amount of change correlated with the starting level? If people who start high show larger declines, the intercept-slope correlation will be negative.

Adding Predictors of Growth

The power of growth curve models comes from asking: what predicts the trajectory? We can add level-2 predictors to explain why some people’s intercepts and slopes differ from others.

Code 
# Add a predictor: treatment condition
df_growth <- df_growth |>
  mutate(treatment = rep(rbinom(n_people, 1, 0.5), each = n_times))

# Treatment might affect both initial level and rate of change
m_growth_pred <- lmer(stress ~ week * treatment + (week | person),
                      data = df_growth)

broom.mixed::tidy(m_growth_pred, effects = "fixed") |>
  knitr::kable(digits = 3,
    caption = "Growth model with treatment: interaction = different slopes by condition")
Growth model with treatment: interaction = different slopes by condition

The week:treatment interaction coefficient tells you whether the treatment group’s stress declines faster or slower than the control group.

Nonlinear Growth

Some processes do not change linearly. Symptoms might improve rapidly at first, then plateau. Performance curves often show diminishing returns. The growth curve framework accommodates nonlinearity.

Code 
# Quadratic growth: 9-week stress relief study
df_growth_q <- expand_grid(person = 1:n_people, week = 0:8) |>
  mutate(
    person_int    = rep(rnorm(n_people, 50, 8), each = 9),
    person_lin    = rep(rnorm(n_people, -5, 2), each = 9),
    person_quad   = rep(rnorm(n_people, 0.5, 0.3), each = 9),
    stress        = person_int + person_lin * week + person_quad * week^2 + rnorm(n(), 0, 3),
    person        = factor(person)
  )

# Fit with quadratic time
m_quad <- lmer(stress ~ week + I(week^2) + (week | person), data = df_growth_q)

# Plot average predicted trajectory
df_growth_q <- df_growth_q |>
  mutate(fitted_stress = fitted(m_quad))

df_growth_q |>
  group_by(week) |>
  summarise(mean_obs = mean(stress), mean_fit = mean(fitted_stress)) |>
  pivot_longer(-week) |>
  ggplot(aes(x = week, y = value, color = name, linetype = name)) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1.2) + geom_point(size = 2.5) +
  scale_color_manual(values = c("#aec6e8", "#1e3a5f"),
                     labels = c("Fitted (quadratic)", "Observed")) +
  scale_linetype_manual(values = c("solid", "dashed"),
                        labels = c("Fitted (quadratic)", "Observed")) +
  labs(title = "Quadratic growth: curve captures the deceleration",
       x = "Week", y = "Mean Stress", color = NULL, linetype = NULL) +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 13)
WarningAssumption: The Growth Function is Correctly Specified

What it means: Linear growth assumes constant rate of change. Quadratic growth assumes a parabolic trajectory. If the true trajectory is neither — e.g., a logarithmic curve or piecewise linear — your model will be misspecified.

How to check: Plot the observed means by time point. Does the pattern look linear? Is there a clear deceleration or acceleration that requires a curve? Use AIC to compare linear vs. quadratic models.

If this fails: Try a quadratic term (I(week^2)), a piecewise model with a knot at a theoretically meaningful wave, or a natural spline (splines::ns(week, df = 3)).

Choosing the Functional Form for Time

The most consequential decision in a growth curve model is how time enters — linear, quadratic, piecewise, or something else. Always choose functional form from the data, not from a significance test.

Now that we have simulated a quadratic dataset (df_growth_q) and a simple linear dataset (df_growth), we can compare how a linear vs. quadratic model fits the quadratic data.

Code 
# Compare functional forms on the quadratic dataset
m_linear_q <- lmer(stress ~ week + (week | person), data = df_growth_q,
                   control = lmerControl(optimizer = "bobyqa"))

anova(m_linear_q, m_quad) |>
  knitr::kable(digits = 3,
    caption = "AIC/BIC and LRT: does adding quadratic time improve fit?")

  theme_minimal(base_size = 12)

Decision rule: upgrade the functional form only when (1) the pre-fit mean-by-wave plot shows curvature AND (2) AIC/BIC clearly favor the more complex model. A quadratic term that just reaches significance with no visible curvature is likely a chance artefact.

Post-Fit Diagnostics

After fitting, check whether residuals show remaining time structure. Remaining pattern means the model has not fully captured the trajectory.

Code 
df_growth <- df_growth |>
  mutate(resid_g = residuals(m_growth))

p_resid_wave <- df_growth |>
  group_by(week) |>
  summarise(mr = mean(resid_g), .groups = "drop") |>
  ggplot(aes(x = week, y = mr)) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 0, linetype = "dashed", color = "grey60") +
  geom_line(color = "#e63946", linewidth = 1.1) +
  geom_point(color = "#e63946", size = 2.5) +
  labs(title = "Mean residuals by wave",
       subtitle = "Remaining pattern = wrong functional form",
       x = "Week", y = "Mean residual") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 11)

p_resid_person <- df_growth |>
  group_by(person) |>
  summarise(mr = mean(resid_g), ms = mean(stress), .groups = "drop") |>
  ggplot(aes(x = ms, y = mr)) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 0, linetype = "dashed", color = "grey60") +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.4, color = "#1e3a5f", size = 2) +
  geom_smooth(method = "lm", se = FALSE, color = "#1e3a5f", linewidth = 0.8) +
  labs(title = "Person-mean residuals vs. mean outcome",
       subtitle = "Slope ≠ 0 = random effects not capturing person differences",
       x = "Person mean stress", y = "Person mean residual") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 11)

WarningAssumption: Random Slope for Time is Correctly Specified

What it means: Allowing only a random intercept (but not slope) for time assumes everyone changes at exactly the same rate. This is almost always wrong for developmental or therapeutic outcomes.

How to check: Compare the random-intercept-only model to the random-slope model using a likelihood ratio test. Plot individual trajectories — if they clearly fan out or diverge, a random slope is needed.

If this fails: Add (week | person) to the model formula. If this causes a singular fit, the data do not support individual slope variation — simplify back to (1 | person) and acknowledge that the model assumes homogeneous rates of change.

Reviewer Challenge

TipWhat a Reviewer Would Ask

Challenge 1: “You fit a linear growth model — but why should stress decline at a constant rate? Isn’t it more plausible that improvement is rapid at first and then decelerates?” The correct response: show the mean-by-wave plot from the pre-fit check. If that plot is approximately linear and AIC does not favor a quadratic model, argue for parsimony. If it shows clear curvature, upgrade to quadratic and show both the comparison and the improvement in residual pattern.

Challenge 2: “You have a random slope for time — does the model converge properly? What is the estimated slope variance and intercept-slope correlation?” Always report: isSingular() result, estimated random slope variance, intercept-slope correlation. If singular, explain what simplification was applied and why.

Challenge 3: “Your growth model uses wave number as time, but participants were measured on different calendar dates. Doesn’t that introduce error into the time variable?” If measurement timing varies across participants, use the exact elapsed time in days or weeks, not a wave label. This is especially important when intervals are unequal or participant-specific.
