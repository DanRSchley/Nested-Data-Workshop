Covariance Structures

NoteThe Structure Question

Which of your repeated observations should look most alike? Adjacent ones — a WTP rating taken immediately after a disclosure is more similar to a rating taken a week later than to a rating taken six months later. This is the starting intuition for choosing a covariance structure.

What does the covariance structure encode? An assumption about the data-generating process — how the underlying psychological or financial state of a person persists, decays, or drifts over time.

What goes wrong if you choose wrong? Standard errors and test statistics are computed using the assumed structure. An incorrect structure produces biased inference — usually overstated precision (too-small SEs) if you assume too little covariance.

The Covariance Matrix Is a Scientific Statement

When you measure the same person at multiple time points, those measurements co-vary. How they co-vary is not a nuisance parameter — it is a substantive statement about the DGP. The choice of covariance structure encodes an assumption about how the process unfolds over time.

A useful starting question: “If I knew Person A’s WTP in week 1, how much does that tell me about their WTP in week 2 versus week 8?” The answer depends on the persistence of the underlying state being measured:

  • Short-lived state (e.g., momentary risk perception after seeing a shocking news headline): week 1 barely predicts week 4. Adjacent observations are only a bit more similar than distant ones.
  • Persistent individual tendency (e.g., stable financial risk tolerance): week 1 predicts week 8 almost as well as week 2. Observations are broadly similar regardless of lag.
  • Gradually decaying memory or habit (e.g., recall of a disclosure or momentum of a savings habit): week 2 is more similar to week 1 than week 8 is, and the similarity decays smoothly with lag.

Each of these stories implies a different covariance structure.

The Main Structures

1. Compound Symmetry (CS)

All variances equal. All covariances equal regardless of lag.

\[\Sigma_{CS} = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma^2 & \sigma^2\rho & \sigma^2\rho \\ \sigma^2\rho & \sigma^2 & \sigma^2\rho \\ \sigma^2\rho & \sigma^2\rho & \sigma^2 \end{pmatrix}\]

The DGP story: Each person has a stable individual level, and every observation is equally predictable from every other. The WTP at month 1 tells you as much about month 6 as it tells you about month 2.

JDM setting: Most appropriate when what you are measuring is a fixed individual trait that shows no dynamic persistence or decay — for example, a stable risk-preference score that does not drift meaningfully across sessions. In practice this is rare; most repeated JDM measurements show some decay structure.

When it fails: Whenever observations are more similar to adjacent ones than to distant ones — which is almost always true in longitudinal JDM studies.

2. Autoregressive Order 1 — AR(1)

Correlations decay exponentially with time lag. Observations one step apart correlate \(\rho\); two steps apart, \(\rho^2\); \(k\) steps apart, \(\rho^k\).

\[\Sigma_{AR1} = \sigma^2\begin{pmatrix} 1 & \rho & \rho^2 \\ \rho & 1 & \rho \\ \rho^2 & \rho & 1 \end{pmatrix}\]

The DGP story: Each observation partially inherits from the previous one. Yesterday’s state predicts today’s, but the influence decays at a constant rate. A financial decision made under a disclosure effect carries partial memory into the next measurement, but fades over longer gaps.

JDM settings: - Daily spending diary: today’s spending predicts tomorrow’s more than next week’s - Weekly savings rate: this week’s savings are correlated with last week’s, but the correlation decays with the lag - Risk perception during a market fluctuation: day-to-day persistence that gradually loses predictive power

When it fits: When your measurement intervals are equal and the process has smooth geometric decay in correlations.

3. Unstructured (UN)

No constraints. Every variance and covariance is estimated freely from the data.

The DGP story: The data speak for themselves. There is no assumed decay pattern or symmetry.

JDM settings: Reasonable when you have a small number of waves (3–4) and a large sample — the model has enough data to estimate every pairwise correlation without exploding the parameter count.

When it fails: With many waves or small samples, the unstructured model is severely overparameterized. With 5 waves and 50 people, you are estimating 15 covariance parameters from data where each person contributes only 5 observations. The estimates are unstable and the model often fails to converge.

4. Toeplitz

Like AR(1) but without the geometric constraint: each lag has its own correlation, but the correlation depends only on the lag size, not the absolute time point.

The DGP story: Lag-1 observations are more similar than lag-2, which are more similar than lag-3 — but the rate of decay does not have to be exponential. A financial decision’s influence might fade quickly at first (week 1 to week 2) and then more slowly (week 3 to week 6), which AR(1) cannot capture but Toeplitz can.

Visualizing the Structures

Code 
make_cov_plot <- function(Sigma, title, subtitle = NULL) {
  n <- nrow(Sigma)
  D <- diag(1 / sqrt(diag(Sigma)))
  Corr <- D %*% Sigma %*% D
  wlabs <- paste0("W", 1:n)

  data.frame(Corr) |>
    setNames(wlabs) |>
    mutate(row = wlabs) |>
    pivot_longer(-row, names_to = "col", values_to = "r") |>
    mutate(row = factor(row, levels = rev(wlabs)),
           col = factor(col, levels = wlabs)) |>
    ggplot(aes(x = col, y = row, fill = r)) +
    geom_tile(color = "white", linewidth = 1) +
    geom_text(aes(label = round(r, 2)), size = 3.5) +
    scale_fill_gradient2(low = "#f7fbff", mid = "#4292c6", high = "#08306b",
                         midpoint = 0.5, limits = c(-0.1, 1)) +
    labs(title = title, subtitle = subtitle, x = NULL, y = NULL) +
    theme_minimal(base_size = 11) +
    theme(legend.position = "none")
}

n <- 5
rho <- 0.6

CS   <- matrix(rho, n, n); diag(CS) <- 1; CS <- CS * 4
AR1  <- outer(0:(n-1), 0:(n-1), function(i, j) rho^abs(i-j)) * 4
Toep <- outer(0:(n-1), 0:(n-1), function(i, j) {
  c(1, 0.7, 0.5, 0.3, 0.2)[abs(i-j)+1]
}) * 4
UN <- matrix(c(4.0, 2.4, 1.8, 1.4, 1.0,
               2.4, 4.2, 2.2, 1.6, 1.1,
               1.8, 2.2, 4.5, 2.4, 1.5,
               1.4, 1.6, 2.4, 4.8, 2.2,
               1.0, 1.1, 1.5, 2.2, 5.0), n, n)

(make_cov_plot(CS,   "Compound Symmetry",
               "Flat correlations — any wave\npredicts any other equally") +
 make_cov_plot(AR1,  "AR(1)",
               "Geometric decay — adjacent\nwaves most similar")) /
(make_cov_plot(Toep, "Toeplitz",
               "Free decay by lag — flexible\nbut still lag-dependent") +
 make_cov_plot(UN,   "Unstructured",
               "No constraints — data-driven\nbut needs large N"))

The heatmaps show how correlation between waves (W1–W5) changes under each structure. AR(1) and Toeplitz capture the realistic pattern where similarity decreases as the time gap grows.

Specifying Covariance Structures in R

lme4 does not support custom covariance structures beyond the random effects structure. For explicit covariance modeling, use nlme:

Code 
# Simulate WTP data from a savings study: 5 waves with AR(1) correlation
n_people <- 50
n_times  <- 5
rho_true <- 0.7

Y_ar1 <- MASS::mvrnorm(n_people, rep(50, n_times),
                        outer(0:(n_times-1), 0:(n_times-1),
                              function(i, j) rho_true^abs(i-j)) * 9)

df_long <- as.data.frame(Y_ar1) |>
  setNames(paste0("wave", 1:n_times)) |>
  mutate(person = 1:n_people) |>
  pivot_longer(-person, names_to = "wave", values_to = "WTP") |>
  mutate(wave_num = as.integer(str_extract(wave, "[0-9]")),
         person   = factor(person))

# Fit four competing models
m_cs  <- gls(WTP ~ factor(wave_num),
             correlation = corCompSymm(form = ~1 | person),
             data = df_long)
m_ar1 <- gls(WTP ~ factor(wave_num),
             correlation = corAR1(form = ~wave_num | person),
             data = df_long)
m_un  <- gls(WTP ~ factor(wave_num),
             correlation = corSymm(form = ~wave_num | person),
             weights = varIdent(form = ~1 | factor(wave_num)),
             data = df_long)

tibble(
  Model = c("Compound Symmetry", "AR(1)", "Unstructured"),
  AIC   = c(AIC(m_cs), AIC(m_ar1), AIC(m_un)),
  BIC   = c(BIC(m_cs), BIC(m_ar1), BIC(m_un))
) |>
  arrange(AIC) |>
  knitr::kable(digits = 1,
    caption = "Model comparison: AR(1) fits best when data has geometric decay in correlations (lower AIC/BIC = better)")
Model comparison: AR(1) fits best when data has geometric decay in correlations (lower AIC/BIC = better)
Model AIC BIC
AR(1) 1132.2 1156.7
Unstructured 1143.6 1213.6
Compound Symmetry 1209.9 1234.4

When the true structure is AR(1), the AR(1) model is preferred by AIC. Compound symmetry underfits (too constrained). Unstructured overfits (too many parameters for this sample size).

Assumptions

WarningAR(1) Assumption: Stationarity

Plain-language meaning: The mean and variance of the outcome are constant across time, and the correlation between any two observations depends only on how far apart they are — not on when in the study they fall. An AR(1) with \(|\phi| < 1\) is stationary by construction.

What the model is assuming away: Trending means (e.g., WTP systematically rising over time due to growing familiarity with the product) or trending variance (e.g., participants becoming more variable later in a long task when fatigue sets in).

JDM example: If participants in a repeated savings task become more financially confident across waves — so WTP increases for all participants regardless of condition — the mean is trending. The AR(1) model will confound this trend with the outcome trajectory unless time is included as a fixed effect.

What a skeptical reviewer might say: “You modeled repeated savings willingness with AR(1) — but if participants are becoming more engaged with the study instrument over time, the mean is trending and stationarity fails.”

Observable warning sign: Plot wave-by-wave means. If the mean increases or decreases systematically across waves, stationarity is violated for the raw outcome. Including wave as a fixed effect typically restores stationarity for the residuals.

What to check: Plot means and variances by wave. Fit a model with time as a fixed effect and check whether residuals are stationary. Use the Augmented Dickey-Fuller test for stationarity on per-person residual series.

What claim survives if the assumption is doubtful: The direction of the condition effect is usually robust. The autoregressive coefficient estimate and its confidence interval may be biased if stationarity is violated.

WarningUnstructured Assumption: Sufficient Sample Size

Plain-language meaning: The unstructured model estimates \(T(T+1)/2\) covariance parameters for \(T\) waves. With 5 waves, that is 15 parameters — just for the covariance structure. These must be estimated from the data.

What the model is assuming away: Any parsimony. The unstructured model places no restrictions on how measurements co-vary.

JDM example: A 6-wave savings diary study with 60 participants requires estimating 21 covariance parameters from a sample that has only 360 observations. That is fewer than 17 observations per covariance parameter — estimates will be noisy and models may fail to converge.

What to check: Compare the number of observations to the number of covariance parameters (aim for at least 10:1). If the unstructured model fails to converge, start with AR(1) and use AIC to evaluate whether the more constrained model fits acceptably.

Model Selection Strategy

Code 
tibble(
  Structure          = c("Compound Symmetry", "AR(1)", "Toeplitz", "Unstructured"),
  `Params (T=5)`    = c("2", "2", "5", "15"),
  `Best for`         = c(
    "Stable trait; no decay; rarely appropriate",
    "Regular intervals; geometric decay; default starting point",
    "Irregular decay; flexible but tractable; good middle ground",
    "Small T; large N; no prior structure; fit check for simpler models"
  )
) |>
  knitr::kable(caption = "Covariance structure selection guide (T = number of waves)")

Compound Symmetry 2 Stable trait; no decay; rarely appropriate

Recommended workflow:

  1. Start with AR(1) as the default for regularly-spaced longitudinal JDM data
  2. Compare to compound symmetry (more constrained) using AIC/BIC — if CS fits as well, use it
  3. Try Toeplitz if the decay looks non-geometric from the raw correlation matrix
  4. Try unstructured only if sample size allows (\(T \leq 4\), large \(N\)) — use as a robustness check
  5. Plot residual autocorrelations after fitting to verify that serial dependence has been removed

Reviewer Challenge

TipWhat a Reviewer Would Ask

Challenge 1: “You fitted a random intercept model with lme4. What covariance structure does that imply for your repeated WTP measurements?” A random intercept model in lme4 implies compound symmetry — all waves correlate equally at \(\rho = \sigma^2_u / (\sigma^2_u + \sigma^2_\varepsilon)\). If adjacent waves should correlate more than distant ones (almost always true), this model misspecifies the covariance structure. Switch to nlme with corAR1() for longitudinal data with meaningful time ordering.

Challenge 2: “Why did you choose AR(1) over unstructured?” The data-driven answer: AIC/BIC favored AR(1) and the unstructured model showed convergence issues (too many parameters for the sample). The conceptual answer: WTP decay after a disclosure intervention is well described by geometric fading — the psychological mechanism supports AR(1) structure.

Challenge 3: “Your AR(1) estimate is \(\hat{\rho} = 0.72\) — doesn’t that suggest the waves are measuring a stable trait rather than a dynamic process?” High AR(1) correlation does not necessarily mean it is a fixed trait. It means the process has strong short-term persistence. A Bayesian risk-tolerance parameter might show \(\rho = 0.72\) across weeks while still responding to information interventions — the AR structure describes the baseline persistence, not the treatment effect.
